## Slide 3: What Is Phishing?
**Talking Points:**
Phishing gets its name from "fishing" — attackers cast bait hoping someone will bite. The bait is usually a fake email, SMS, or call. The hook is a malicious link or request for information. The scary thing is how effective it is: 91% of all cyberattacks begin with a phishing email. That's not a small statistic — that means if your organization is ever breached, it almost certainly started with someone clicking something they shouldn't have.

**Interactive Prompt:** *"Has anyone here ever received what you think was a phishing email? What did it look like?"*

**Estimated Time:** 4 minutes

## Slide 4: What Attackers Want
**Talking Points:**
Let's think like a hacker for a moment. If you could trick someone into clicking a link, what would you want? Their passwords? Their credit card? Control of their computer? Attackers want all of these things. Password theft is the most common, because with your email password they can then reset every other password you have. Financial data is valuable on its own. And installing malware gives them ongoing access to your system — sometimes for months before they're detected.

**Estimated Time:** 3 minutes

---

## Slide 5: Types of Phishing
**Talking Points:**
Most people know about email phishing, but there are five major types. Spear phishing is particularly dangerous because it's targeted — the attacker knows your name, your company, maybe even your recent projects. Whaling targets CEOs and CFOs — high-value targets. Smishing via SMS is growing rapidly because people's guard is lower when texting. And vishing by phone is effective because human voice creates a false sense of authenticity. Ask yourself: which of these have you experienced?

**Estimated Time:** 5 minutes

## Slide 6: Statistics
**Talking Points:**
These numbers are not hypothetical. In 2023 alone, phishing attacks cost $4.9 billion. The average time before someone clicks a phishing link is 20 seconds — attackers know this. They know that after 20 seconds, you might think twice. That's why every phishing message is designed to make you act in the first few seconds. The key defense? **Pause before you click.** Those extra 10 seconds of thought can save you — and your organization — from catastrophe.

**Estimated Time:** 3 minutes

## Slide 7: Anatomy of a Phishing Email
**Talking Points:**
This is the most important slide in the deck. Walk through the fake PayPal email with me. Look at the sender address first — `paypall-verify.com`. That extra 'l' is a typosquat. Real PayPal emails come from `@paypal.com`. Next, the subject line — "URGENT: Your account will be SUSPENDED." That's designed to make you panic. The greeting is generic — no name. The body creates a 24-hour deadline. And that big red button? It would link to a fake PayPal login page designed to steal your credentials. Every element is engineered to bypass your critical thinking.

**Estimated Time:** 6 minutes

## Slide 8: Fake Website Detection
**Talking Points:**
Here's a trick that will save you: the golden rule of URL checking. Find the first `/` in the URL after the `https://`. Everything before that first slash is the domain. That's what matters. `paypal-secure.verify-login.com` — the domain is `verify-login.com`, not PayPal. `microsoft365-login.com` — the domain is `microsoft365-login.com`, not Microsoft. Another thing: HTTPS and the padlock icon do NOT mean the site is legitimate. Phishing sites have SSL certificates too. It only means the connection is encrypted.

**Estimated Time:** 5 minutes

## Slide 9: Social Engineering Tactics
**Talking Points:**
Social engineering is about hacking people, not computers. Attackers are essentially psychologists who exploit human cognitive shortcuts. The six tactics on this slide are used in virtually every phishing attack. Urgency and scarcity work together to push you to act before thinking. Authority exploits our trained tendency to comply with people in power. Fear is perhaps the most effective — we are wired to respond to threats faster than opportunities. Recognition is the first line of defense: when you feel that urgent, fearful pressure, that's exactly when to slow down and be skeptical.

**Estimated Time:** 5 minutes

---

## Slide 10: Psychology
**Talking Points:**
Daniel Kahneman, a Nobel Prize-winning psychologist, described two modes of thinking: System 1 (fast, instinctive, emotional) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, logical). Phishing is designed to activate System 1. The urgency, the fear, the authority — they all bypass System 2. The countermeasure is deliberately engaging System 2. I call it the "10-second rule" — before clicking anything in an email, take 10 seconds and ask: did I expect this? Does the sender look right? Is this urgent for a real reason?

**Estimated Time:** 4 minutes

## Slide 11: Malware
**Talking Points:**
Malware delivered through phishing is one of the most devastating outcomes. Ransomware has shut down hospitals — literally putting patients at risk. The 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack started with a single compromised password and led to fuel shortages across the Eastern US. Keyloggers are invisible — you'd never know one is running. It just silently records everything: your banking logins, your company credentials, personal messages. The lesson: never download attachments or click "enable macros" from unexpected emails.

**Estimated Time:** 4 minutes

---

## Slide 12: Credential Theft
**Talking Points:**
The credential theft process is elegant and brutal. A fake login page is pixel-perfect — it looks exactly like the real site. You enter your password. The fake site logs your credentials, then silently redirects you to the real site so you think "oh, it worked." You never know what happened. Within hours, your account is being used, your password changed, and the attacker is inside your other accounts using the same password you reused. This is why unique passwords for every account are non-negotiable.

**Estimated Time:** 4 minutes

---

## Slide 13: MFA
**Talking Points:**
Multi-Factor Authentication is the single most impactful security measure you can enable today. Microsoft reports that MFA prevents 99.9% of automated attacks. Even if an attacker has your password, they can't log in without your phone. For most people, an authenticator app is the best balance of security and convenience — Google Authenticator, Authy, or Microsoft Authenticator. SMS OTPs are better than nothing but can be intercepted. Hardware keys like YubiKey are the gold standard — they are completely phishing-resistant.

**Interactive Prompt:** *"How many of you have MFA enabled on your primary email account? Raise your hand."*

**Estimated Time:** 5 minutes

## Slide 14: Password Hygiene
**Talking Points:**
The password "Password123" is cracked in under a second by automated tools. Yet it — and variations of it — remain among the most common passwords used. The most secure approach is a password manager. You remember one strong master password, and it generates and stores unique, complex passwords for every site. If that site gets breached, only that one account is compromised. Strong passphrases — four or more random words strung together — are also excellent because length beats complexity.

**Estimated Time:** 4 minutes

---

## Slide 15: Safe Browsing & Reporting
**Talking Points:**
Reporting phishing is not just for your own protection — it protects everyone. When you report a phishing email in Gmail or Outlook, it helps train the AI filters that protect millions of other users. Most organizations have a dedicated phishing report button or email address. Use it. One report can stop the same email from reaching hundreds of others. Also: bookmark sites you use frequently — banking, email, work tools. Never navigate to sensitive sites from links in emails.

## Slide 18: Defense Playbook
**Talking Points:**
These 10 rules are your personal security policy. Print them out. Put them on your monitor. Make them habit. The most important: never click email links for sites with sensitive information — always type the URL directly. And rule 10 is the one people forget: keep training. Attackers evolve constantly. The phishing emails of 2024 are far more convincing than those from 2019. Security awareness is not a one-time training — it's a continuous practice.
